"We want to do something custom with AI" is one of the sentences we hear most often on a first call. It almost never comes with a problem behind it. It comes with a hunch: that competitors are already doing it, that generic tools fall short, that there must be a better way to do what is done by hand today.

The hunch is usually right. What is missing is turning it into a project with a scope, a timeline and a price, and designing it so that it reaches production instead of staying a demo. That is what this article is about.

## The short answer

A custom AI project is a system built for one specific process in your company, connected to your tools and your data, in which a language model does only the part that requires judgement: reading, classifying, drafting or proposing a decision. The rest of the process is fixed rules. It is not training your own model or developing an application from scratch.

## What a custom AI project is, and what it is not

Four misunderstandings are worth clearing up before we get to examples, because each one multiplies the budget for no reason.

**It is not training your own model.** The models from OpenAI, Anthropic or Google already read, summarise and write better than a model trained from scratch by a mid-sized company ever could. What gets built to measure is everything around them: where they get their information, which rules they have to follow, which system they write to and who reviews what they do.

**It is not starting with an application.** Today anyone can have an app running in an afternoon with Lovable or Claude Code, and that does not solve the problem of almost any company. Most projects that work have no new screens. They work inside what the team already uses: the CRM, the inbox, WhatsApp, the order sheet. What gets built is workflows, integrations and agents, usually on tools such as [n8n](https://docs.n8n.io/advanced-ai/ "follow"), Make or the model's API. Sometimes the process does call for a small piece of software, like the application that generates the proposals at Eventos Barcelona, and then it is built as one more part of the system, not as an end in itself.

**It is not a chatbot on the website.** A chatbot is one of the possible forms, and rarely the most profitable one. The biggest return is in processes nobody sees from outside: what happens between a contact coming in and someone replying, or between a document arriving and someone typing it into another system.

**It is not replacing anyone.** The projects that turn out best take the repetitive part of a person's job away and leave them the part that needs judgement. The ones that try to remove human judgement from a process that needs it end up with someone checking by hand everything the machine does.

## Custom, configured or off the shelf?

Before commissioning a custom project, rule out the two cheaper options. This table sums up when each one fits:

| Option | What it is | When it fits | When it falls short |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Off-the-shelf tool** | Software that already includes the AI feature (the CRM assistant, an invoice reader, a WhatsApp chatbot) | The process is standard and the tool covers almost everything you need | Your process has steps of its own or has to talk to systems the tool does not integrate |
| **Configured automation** | Rules in the CRM or in a connector such as Make, [Zapier](https://help.zapier.com/hc/en-us/articles/8496342944013-Create-AI-powered-actions-with-AI-by-Zapier "follow") or n8n, with the odd AI step | The process crosses two or three tools and its decisions fit into rules | There is a lot of free text to interpret, many exceptions or expensive errors |
| **Custom AI project** | A system designed for your process, with AI at the decision points, validations and human review | The process is frequent, touches several systems, needs judgement and costs money when it fails | Volume is low or the process is not yet defined |

If an off-the-shelf tool covers what you need and the process is not what sets you apart, buy it: it is cheaper and someone else maintains it. We cover the families of tools and what each one solves in [AI for automating processes: which tools to use](/en/blog/herramientas-ia-para-automatizar-procesos).

### What if I can already build it myself with Lovable?

With Lovable, Bolt or Claude Code, a person with no technical team can have an application running in an afternoon, and that makes it look as if building is now cheap. Writing the first version is. Everything else costs what it always did: deciding what to build, integrating it with what already exists, testing it with real data, deploying it, maintaining it when dependencies change and fixing it when it fails on a Tuesday afternoon. Writing code is a fraction of the total effort, and halving the cost of that fraction does not halve the cost of the project.

The question that decides it is not whether it can be built, because almost anything can. It is whether the thing you need is what sets you apart from your competitors. If it is, building makes sense. If not, buying almost always wins: a ready-made CRM or invoicing tool has years of edge cases already solved, and the annoying detail that pushes you to build your own version costs far less than maintaining it for three years.

## Five examples of custom AI projects

These are the types of project that come up most often in service companies. Two of them are our own cases in production.

### 1. From email to proposal, without touching it by hand

This is the case of [Eventos Barcelona](/en/casos-de-exito/eventos-barcelona). Each proposal took between one and three days to go out, and by then the client had already asked others for a quote. Now the system reads every incoming email, decides whether it comes from a client, an artist or a supplier, opens or updates the opportunity in the CRM and, after the call, generates the proposal with the company's branding as a web page and a PDF, sends it and schedules the follow-up.

The proposal goes out about **eight minutes** after the call, and response time to the client dropped by **85%**. The AI writes the parts that change from one client to the next; prices and terms come from rules, not from the model. We explain it in detail in [automatic sales proposals](/en/blog/propuestas-comerciales-automaticas).

### 2. An agent that replies and qualifies on WhatsApp

Every message that arrives by WhatsApp, form or email is read, classified by need and fit, logged in the CRM and answered with the next step: a question, a booking link or a note that a person will call. The AI comes in because messages arrive as free text: "hi, I wanted info about the thing for my mother" does not fit any box. The agent hands the conversation to a person as soon as there is buying intent or something goes off script. We cover it in [how to qualify leads automatically](/en/blog/como-cualificar-leads-automaticamente) and in the [AI agent for WhatsApp](/en/servicios/agente-ia-whatsapp) service.

### 3. Document reading

Invoices, orders, delivery notes, contracts or work reports that arrive as a PDF or a photo and that someone types by hand into another system. The model extracts the fields, the system validates them against rules (that the tax ID exists, that the amount matches the order) and only what does not fit goes to human review. It is a rewarding project because the time saved can be measured from day one.

### 4. Prospecting and outbound with AI

Building the list of companies that fit, enriching each contact with public data (size, technology in use, recent hires) and writing a first message that talks about their situation and not about your product. Tools such as Clay, Instantly or Valley each do one piece separately; the custom project is the system that joins them to your CRM, decides who to write to and when, and returns the replies to the sales team with context. We explain it in [what GTM Engineering is](/en/blog/que-es-gtm-engineering) and in the [B2B outbound](/en/servicios/outbound-b2b) service. We compare the enrichment tools in [Clay, Treg or ColdIQ](/en/blog/clay-treg-coldiq-enriquecimiento-prospeccion-b2b) and explain how to set it up in [B2B prospecting with AI](/en/blog/prospeccion-b2b-outbound-con-ia).

### 5. An agent that acts with human approval

A system that watches something continuously and proposes actions. At [Tribeca Media](/en/casos-de-exito/tribeca-media) we built the Performance Agent for their KEYWORKERS platform: it reviews the Meta and Google Ads campaigns, proposes pausing ads, lowering budget or creating new campaigns, and generates a new creative when a Meta ad wears out. No action runs without a person's sign-off, approvals expire after 24 hours, raising budget is never automatic and each client's AI cost has a daily cap.

That is the golden rule for any agent: it proposes and a person approves, at least until the track record shows that it gets things right. We explain when it pays off and when it does not in [AI agents for companies](/en/blog/agentes-de-ia-para-empresas-cuando-compensan).

## AI projects in production: why so many stay at the pilot stage

A demo that works does not mean the system will hold up on Monday morning with real data. Almost every project that fails to reach production trips over the same four stones:

**The AI is given the whole flow.** The more steps an agent chains on its own, the more mistakes it makes, and [the errors pile up](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents "follow") without anyone seeing them. At Eventos Barcelona, of the eight steps from the email to the proposal follow-up, the AI decides only two: what type of sender it is and whether the email is about a new project or an open one. Cleaning the email, finding the contact, opening the opportunity, generating the proposal, sending it and chasing it are rules. When the AI gets it wrong, the error is small and visible.

**There are no baseline numbers.** If nobody measured how many cases came in, how long they took and how many were lost, there is no way to prove the project works, and it gets cancelled after the first bad month. We explain how to measure it in a week in [measuring a process before automating it](/en/blog/medir-un-proceso-antes-de-automatizarlo).

**There is no manual way out.** Every system needs a route for a person to step in when something falls outside what was planned. Without it, the first odd case breaks the team's trust and three weeks later everyone is back to doing it by hand.

**Nobody watches the cost or the failures.** A system in production needs alerts when something fails and a record of how much it spends on AI. At Tribeca, before the agent was allowed to act, we built health checks, a log of the cost of every call and a spending cap per client.

## The phases and how long each one takes

A well-run AI project goes through four phases. The one that is most underestimated is the first.

| Phase | Duration | What comes out of it |
|---|---|---|
| **Discovery** | 1 to 2 weeks | The process mapped as it is today, its baseline numbers (volume, time and leakage) and the scope agreed in writing |
| **Build** | 2 to 4 weeks | Integrations, rules, the AI part, validations and alerts |
| **Testing with real data** | 1 to 2 weeks | The system runs in parallel with the manual process and results are compared; the odd cases show up |
| **Go-live and tuning** | 2 to 4 weeks | The team uses it for real, instructions and thresholds are adjusted and it is measured against the baseline numbers |

In total, between four and eight weeks until the first process runs on its own. If someone promises two, they are counting the build and leaving out everything else. A first visible result usually arrives earlier, at the end of the build, but it should not be mistaken for a finished system.

Within the implementation range, the project lands higher or lower depending on its size:

- **Low end: a scoped process inside one system.** Qualifying contacts that come in through the website and putting them in the pipeline with their score. One tool, clear rules, few exceptions.
- **Middle: a process that crosses several systems.** From the email to the proposal sent, via calendar and CRM. The price is set by how many integrations there are.
- **High end: a system with agents that act.** Several integrations, AI that proposes actions on real systems, human approval, a log of every action and cost control.

What moves the price within each band is almost always the same: **how many systems have to be connected**, **how many exceptions the process has**, **how much a mistake costs** (if an error is a wrongly issued invoice, you need validations and human review) and **what state the data is in** (if it lives in spreadsheets that everyone fills in their own way, they have to be cleaned first).

What usually costs less than people imagine is the use of the AI model itself. At the volumes of a mid-sized company, the API bill is a small figure next to building and maintaining the system, as long as AI is used only where it is needed.

## How to work out whether it pays off, before asking for a quote

You can do this yourself this afternoon. Take the process and get three numbers:

- **Frequency**: how many times it happens per week.
- **Time**: how many minutes it takes each time, from start to finish.
- **Cost per hour** of whoever does it, using their real cost to the company, not their gross salary divided by hours.

Multiply the three and you have the annual cost of carrying on doing it by hand. If that number does not reach what it would cost to automate it, the answer is not to automate it, and that is a perfectly good answer.

But in sales processes the money is almost never in the hours saved. It is in what you stop losing: the contact who does not get a reply in time and goes elsewhere, the proposal that arrives when the client has already decided, the appointment nobody confirms. To put a number on it you need two more figures: **how many contacts come in per month** and **how many are lost without reaching a real conversation**. Almost nobody knows the second one, so you have to count it: how many contacts from last month never got to talk to anyone. Multiply the lost contacts by your close rate and by your average deal value, and you have the annual cost of the leak. Compare it with the hours saved: in a sales process it is usually the number that decides whether the project makes sense.

## What to ask for before signing

It does not matter who you do it with. Before accepting a quote for custom AI, check that the provider gives you in writing:

- **The process that will be automated, with its baseline numbers.** If there are no numbers from before, there will be no way to know whether it worked.
- **What the AI decides and what is a rule.** If the answer is "the AI does everything", the system will be hard to debug and expensive to maintain.
- **Who owns what is built and where it lives.** The reasonable arrangement is for the workflows, the code and the tool accounts to be in your name, so that you can change provider without starting from zero.
- **What happens when the model gets it wrong.** There must be a manual way out, alerts and someone who finds out.
- **What maintenance includes.** [Models change version](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/model-deprecations "follow"), third-party APIs change their terms and processes evolve. A system without maintenance starts to degrade within months.
- **What happens if something changes in three months and it stops working.** If the answer is a new quote, today's price is not the price. A quote far below the others usually leaves out defining the process, testing with real cases or maintenance.
- **How personal data is handled.** Which data goes out to the model provider, [under which contract](https://www.boe.es/buscar/doc.php?id=DOUE-L-2016-80807 "follow") and in which region. We go through it in [GDPR and automation with AI](/en/blog/rgpd-y-automatizacion-con-ia).

## How to tell whether your company needs a custom project

Three questions settle it in most cases:

1. **Is there a tool that already does it and that you could configure?** If the answer is yes, configure it and automate around it. It is cheaper and someone else maintains it.
2. **Does the process repeat often enough to pay for the build?** Something that happens twice a month almost never does.
3. **Does it need to read, classify or write free text?** If every decision can be written as a rule, what you need is an automation, not an AI project.

If you answer no, yes and yes, you probably have a good candidate. If you want the full plan, from choosing the process to the team using it, it is in [how to implement AI in your company](/en/blog/como-implementar-ia-en-tu-empresa).
