Almost every company that calls us has tried to automate something before. [A Zap](https://help.zapier.com/hc/en-us/articles/8496309697421-Create-Zaps "follow") that connected the form to a spreadsheet. A chatbot installed on a Tuesday and uninstalled on the Thursday. An email automation that keeps sending messages to people who are already customers.

The pattern repeats: the tool did not fail, the choice of process did.

Automating well does not start with picking software. It starts with deciding what is worth automating, and that is a business question, not a technology one.

## The short answer

AI process automation means that a company process runs by itself every time it happens, with rules to move the data and trigger actions, and with an AI model only in the steps that need to read or write free text. To start, choose a process that meets four conditions at once: it happens often, it follows stable rules, it costs measurable time and its failure has consequences. In most SMEs that process sits between the moment a contact arrives and the moment someone does something with it. Measure volume, time and leakage before touching anything, centralise, automate the obvious and only then add AI. A well-scoped first process is implemented in four to six weeks.

If your job is to decide how to bring AI into the whole company, and not just into one process, start with [how to implement AI in your company](/en/blog/como-implementar-ia-en-tu-empresa).

## Which processes can be automated with AI

Almost any process that repeats and receives unstructured information (messages, emails, forms with free text, documents) can be automated with AI. In an SME, these are the most common:

- **Sales and lead capture**: reply at once to every contact that arrives, qualify it, book the call and follow up until they answer.
- **Proposals and quotes**: generate the proposal from the call data and send an alert if the client does not open it.
- **Customer service**: classify incoming messages, answer the repeated questions and hand over to a person whatever needs judgement.
- **Scheduling**: confirm, remind and reschedule appointments without anyone making a call.
- **Administration**: read invoices, delivery notes or documents and pass the data to the right system.
- **Management**: prepare the weekly summary of what came in, what was closed and what was lost.

We go through them one by one, with what the automation does and where the AI comes in, in [fifteen examples of processes an SME can automate](/en/blog/ejemplos-automatizacion-procesos-con-ia). That something can be automated does not mean it is the right place to start. To choose the first one, use the four conditions below.

## Why the first project is almost always chosen badly

When someone decides to automate, they usually pick one of three things: the most visible, the most annoying or the most modern.

The most visible is usually the website or the chat, because it can be seen from outside. The most annoying is usually that task someone hates doing on Fridays. And the most modern is whatever appeared on LinkedIn that week.

None of the three criteria has anything to do with return.

A process is worth automating when it meets four conditions at once: it happens often, it follows stable rules, it costs measurable time and its failure has consequences. If any of the four is missing, the project gets more complicated than it pays back.

## The four conditions, one by one

**It happens often.** Automating something that happens twice a month almost never pays off. The cost of building and maintaining it eats the saving. Frequency is the first thing to look at, and it is worth measuring it properly instead of estimating it: the perception of how often a task repeats is usually well off.

**It follows stable rules.** If the process changes every time depending on the judgement of whoever does it, it is not ready to be automated. It is ready to be documented. Many projects that look like automation projects are really definition projects: until someone decides what the criterion is, there is nothing to program.

**It costs measurable time.** It is not enough for it to be tedious. It has to consume hours that can be counted. If nobody knows how much time goes into it, the project will have no way to show that it worked.

**Its failure has consequences.** A lead that gets no reply is lost. An invoice that is not issued gets paid late. An appointment that is not confirmed becomes an empty slot in the calendar. When failure costs money, the automation pays for itself.

To compare candidates, it helps to put them in a table and answer yes or no to each condition. The one with four yeses goes first; with three, you can consider whether the failing one can be fixed beforehand; with two or fewer, not yet.

| Candidate process | Does it happen often? | Stable rules? | Measurable time? | Does its failure cost money? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Replying to and following up on incoming contacts | Yes, daily | Yes, if they are defined | Yes | Yes: each failure is a lost sale |
| Preparing proposals or quotes | Yes, every week | Almost always | Yes | Yes: being late means losing the sale |
| Confirming and reminding appointments | Yes, daily | Yes | Yes | Yes: each no-show is a paid empty slot |
| Monthly report for management | No, once a month | Yes | Little | Rarely |
| Handling complex complaints | It depends | No, each case is different | Difficult | Yes, but it needs human judgement |

## Where the first project almost always is

In most small and medium-sized businesses, the process that meets the four conditions is in the same place: between the moment a contact arrives and the moment someone does something with it.

Almost everything concentrates there. Forms, WhatsApp messages, calls and emails come in. Someone has to look at them, decide whether they are worth it, reply, book and follow up. It happens daily, the rules are stable if they are defined, it consumes hours and each failure is a sale that does not happen.

That is why the first project we propose is almost always that one, and not the most eye-catching. Not because it is the most interesting technically, but because it is where the return is fastest and easiest to demonstrate.

## A real case: the proposals at Eventos Barcelona

Eventos Barcelona organises events for companies. Its bottleneck was exactly in that stretch between contact and action: after each call with a client, the proposal took between one and three days to go out. By then, the client had already asked other suppliers for a quote.

The process met the four conditions:

- **It happened often**: every sales call ended in a proposal.
- **It followed stable rules**: type of service, dates, capacity, city and margin. What changed was the data, not the criterion.
- **It cost measurable time**: calendar days and hours of layout work per proposal.
- **Its failure had consequences**: in events, whoever replies first has a real advantage, and being late meant losing the event.

We built it in the order of this article. First, everything in one place: [a proposal pipeline in GoHighLevel](https://help.gohighlevel.com/support/solutions/articles/155000001982-understanding-pipelines "follow") with its status (sent, opened, in negotiation, accepted or rejected). Then, the obvious: when the call ends, the salesperson fills in a form that takes three to five minutes and the system generates the proposal as a web page and as a PDF with the company's branding, sends it within a minute and logs it in the CRM. Finally, the follow-up: if the client does not open it within 48 hours, a reminder; if they open it and do not reply within 72, an alert to the salesperson.

The result: the proposal goes out about 8 minutes after hanging up, instead of one to three days, and the response time to the client dropped by 85%. The important thing is not the number, but that it could be measured because we knew how long it took before.

## What to measure before touching anything

This step is almost always skipped and it is the one that decides whether the project can be defended afterwards.

Before automating, you need three numbers from the current process:

- **Volume**: how many times it happens per week.
- **Time**: how long each one takes, from start to finish.
- **Leakage**: how many are lost along the way, and at what point.

With those three numbers, the return calculates itself. Without them, any improvement will be a feeling, and feelings do not survive the first bad month.

The third is the most uncomfortable to look at and the most valuable. In a sales process without a system, part of the contacts that come in are lost, and almost nobody knows how many. Not because anyone wants to lose them, but because nobody has a list of who is still pending.

How to get each number in a week, without tools and with a worked example, is explained in [how to measure a process before automating it](/en/blog/medir-un-proceso-antes-de-automatizarlo).

## What role AI really plays

It helps to separate two things that get mixed up all the time: automation and AI.

**Automation** moves data and triggers actions according to rules. If a form arrives, create the contact, assign it, send the email, schedule the reminder. It needs no AI at all, and in fact it is most of the work in any serious project.

**AI** is used where judgement is needed: reading free text and understanding what it asks for, classifying a request that does not fit in a box, writing a tailored reply, summarising a long conversation.

The expensive mistake is to use AI where a rule was enough. It is [slower, more expensive, less predictable](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents "follow") and harder to debug. If the decision can be written as "if this happens, do that", write it that way.

AI adds value when the input data is unstructured. A WhatsApp message typed by hand, a three-paragraph email, a description of a problem. A rule cannot handle that and a model can.

Which tools cover each part (CRM, connectors such as Zapier, [Make](https://developers.make.com/api-documentation/api-reference/scenarios "follow") or [n8n](https://docs.n8n.io/ "follow"), and models via API) is reviewed in [which tools to use in each case](/en/blog/herramientas-ia-para-automatizar-procesos).

## The order that works

After quite a few implementations, the order that causes the fewest problems is this:

**First, centralise.** Before automating anything, everything has to arrive in one place. If the contacts live in four different inboxes, no automation will cope with that. This phase looks unimpressive and it is the one that changes things the most. That place is usually a CRM; if you are evaluating one, see [what GoHighLevel is and when it pays off](/en/blog/que-es-gohighlevel).

**Second, automate the obvious.** The actions nobody argues about: create the record, acknowledge receipt, assign, remind. No judgement, no AI, no sophistication. This already removes most of the leakage.

**Third, add judgement.** Qualification, prioritisation, tailored replies. This is where AI comes in, and it comes in on top of a process that already works and is already measured.

**Fourth, expand.** When the first one is stable and proven, the next process. Never two at once at the beginning.

Reversing this order is the most common cause of projects that are left half done. Putting an AI agent on top of a process that is neither centralised nor measured is building the roof before the walls.

## The three most expensive mistakes

**Automating a broken process.** An automation does not fix a badly designed process, it speeds it up. If the current process loses customers, automating it loses them faster and in larger numbers.

**Leaving no manual way out.** Every automatic system needs a path for a person to step in when something falls outside what was planned. Without that path, the first odd case breaks the team's trust and three weeks later everyone is back to doing it by hand.

**Not measuring afterwards.** If the three numbers from before are not compared with the ones from after, the project can be neither defended nor improved. And the first month that sales drop for any reason, the automation will take the blame.

## Where to start tomorrow

Take the process through which money enters your business. Write on a sheet every step from the moment someone shows interest until they buy. Mark which ones a person does by hand.

Of those, mark the ones that repeat daily and follow rules you could explain to someone new in five minutes.

That is your first project. No more analysis is needed to start.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is AI process automation?

It means making a company process run by itself every time it happens: rules that move the data and trigger actions, and an AI model in the steps that need to interpret free text, such as a message, an email or a document. Most of the work is classic automation; AI is used where a rule falls short.

### Which processes can be automated with AI?

Those that repeat and receive unstructured information: replying to and following up on contacts, preparing proposals, classifying and answering customer messages, confirming appointments, reading invoices or documents and preparing summaries for management. Whatever needs human judgement in each case, such as a complex complaint, is best left for later.

### How do you automate a process with AI?

In four steps and in this order: centralise the information in one place, normally a CRM; automate the obvious actions with rules (create the record, acknowledge receipt, assign, remind); add AI only in the steps that need to read or write text; and measure before and after to know whether it has worked.

### What is the best AI for automating processes?

There is no single one. What works is a combination: a CRM where the information lives, a connector such as Zapier, Make or n8n that moves the data, and a language model (from OpenAI, Anthropic or Google) that is called only in the steps that need it. We compare them in [which tools to use in each case](/en/blog/herramientas-ia-para-automatizar-procesos).

### How much does an AI automation cost?

At Rianex, discovery starts at €1,500 and the full implementation of one process ranges from €5,000 to €25,000, plus tool licences and AI model usage, which depend on volume. The detail is in [the price of a custom AI project](/en/blog/proyectos-de-ia-a-medida-ejemplos-plazos-precio).

### Which process should be automated first?

The one that happens often, follows stable rules, costs measurable time and whose failure has consequences. In most SMEs it is replying to and following up on incoming contacts, because each failure is a sale that does not happen.

### How long does it take to automate the first process?

Between four and six weeks for a well-scoped process, including the phase of defining how it should work, which is usually the slowest.

### Do you need AI to automate a process?

Not always. If every decision can be written as a rule, automation is enough. AI pays off when the information arrives unstructured and judgement is needed to read it, classify it or answer it.

### What should be measured before automating?

Three numbers from the current process: how many times it happens per week, how long each one takes and how many are lost along the way. Without them there is no way to show that the automation has worked.
